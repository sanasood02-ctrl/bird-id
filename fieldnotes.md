When birdwatching, here are some distinguishing traits, or “field marks,” to focus on while you’re trying to identify species.

Size and shape: Choose a known bird as a reference size, and roughly estimate the size of your mystery bird using it. For example, “larger than a sparrow, smaller than a pigeon,” “same size as a crow,” etc. Additionally, focus on the shape of its body and features. Examine the overall proportions of the bird — for example, “long neck,” “stout body,” etc.

Colour pattern: Pay attention to the main colours of the bird, as well as specific plumage patterns, such as eye rings, stripes, crests, and wing or tail markings. Separately, note the colour of bare parts like the beak and feet, since these can be just as distinctive as the plumage itself.

Beak type: The beak’s shape and size often point straight to a bird’s diet and family. Note whether it’s thin and pointed (insect-eaters), short and conical (seed-eaters), hooked (birds of prey), flat and broad (dabbling ducks), or long and thin (wading birds, hummingbirds). Also note the beak’s relative length — short and stubby versus long and slender.

Foot type: Feet reveal how and where a bird lives. Is it webbed (swimming), has long thin toes (wading in mud or walking on lily pads), has strong curved talons (gripping prey), or has feet built for perching, with three toes forward and one back? Noting foot type can quickly rule out whole categories of birds.

Wingspan and flight pattern: If you catch the bird in flight, note the wing shape — broad and rounded, long and pointed, or short and rounded — along with the flight style: soaring, flapping steadily, swooping in an undulating pattern, or hovering in place. Wingspan relative to body size (e.g., “wings seem too big for its body”) is also a useful clue.

Other characteristics: Anything else distinctive is worth jotting down — behavior (tail-bobbing, hopping vs. walking, perching low vs. high), habitat (near water, in dense brush, open field), vocalizations (even a rough phonetic guess, like “a sharp chip-chip”), and the season or time of day you spotted it. No single trait needs to be conclusive — the combination is what narrows it down.

You don’t need all of these for every bird — even two or three solid field marks are often enough for an ID.
